  Written on the morning of the funerals of a Snooky's mother, Angie, and Craig's
  uncle, Lorrie. Snooky messaged to say he was walking in the sun listening
  to Einaudi's Melodia Africana — the music I listened to after my own mother
  died. I sat in the dining room with it playing, drinking the tea Lissy taught
  me (much like the one I once made for her), and looked around: my mother's clock, 
  Lissy's dream catcher, the chimes, the tapestry from India, the painting of our 
  wedding venue, the map of that day. Somber, but comforting. Everything so large and
  endless, apart from our lives. But all of it connected.

[Einaudi playing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t8h_2GAlPr8), the kettle cooling,  
a cup of turmeric, ginger, lemon,  
cayenne and the black pepper's small fire —  
Lissy's recipe, carried back across the world  
to the house where I once made it for her.  
That's how care travels:  
it leaves, and it learns the way home.

Above the mantel, my mother's clock  
keeps the time she gave me.  
She knew the boy who wondered  
what time was made of,  
and she answered with a gift  
that's still answering.

Beneath it, a dream catcher —  
Lissy's hands this time,  
though the habit is my mother's,  
who wove them for her grandchildren  
the year our house came apart,  
when our youngest woke in wet sheets  
and all she could do was knot a net  
for whatever frightened them in the dark.  
Sometimes love is only that:  
a circle of string  
held up against the night.

Behind it, the chimes.  
I love music,  
but this is the sound I'd keep  
if I could keep only one —  
the air, touched lightly,  
remembering it was music all along.

Across the room, a tapestry from India.  
Three of us went. Two travelled on.  
I came home and learned  
that distance is a kind of thread too.

Behind me, a lake, a pergola,  
a swing hung from a tree,  
painted by a friend's mother  
who gave us our wedding twice —  
once as a day,  
once as a place to keep it.  
Beside it, the map of that day,  
where the door opened  
and everyone stood in the same light:  
the ones who came before,  
the ones still on their way.

Today two families bury their own.  
A mother. An uncle.  
My friend walks in the sun  
with this same music in his ears,  
and I sit here with mine,  
and the song is a bridge  
neither of us crosses alone.

Soon there will be a baby in this house.  
Summer is six months old across a sea.  
The lullabies are written.  
The clock is wound.

Everything is so large this morning,  
so wide, so without end —  
except us. We are small,  
the briefest things in the room.

But look what the brief leave behind:  
a clock, a cup, a net of string,  
a painted swing, a song for someone  
who hasn't yet arrived.  
Nothing here was bought for its own sake.  
Everything in this room was given.

And what is given  
goes on giving  
long after the hands are gone.
